## Framing: what this conversation actually is

He is not choosing between a salary and a startup. He is choosing between **his project and this one**. That changes everything about how you pitch.

- Don't sell "startups are exciting." He knows.
- Don't sell the idea in the abstract. Sell **why this one has a dated inflection behind it and a specific unanswered question in front of it** — two things his other project probably doesn't have.
- Don't oversell. He's smart and he'll check things on his phone. The honesty is the persuasion; there is a list of banned claims at the bottom of this page and breaking one of them costs more than any argument wins.
- He's already partly in. So this isn't "let me explain my business" — it's "here's where it stands, here's what I need, here's the number." Open with permission to skip: *"stop me on anything you already know."*

## Run sheet (~80 minutes)

### 0:00–0:10 — Where it actually stands

Status update, not a pitch. Numbers and bad news in the same breath.

- Audit tool works. M&O Living: 86 products, **16.8/100** spec-query retrievability. Six attributes buyers search on — care instructions, indoor/outdoor, removable covers, load capacity, assembly, fabric durability — appear on **zero** products.
- **Say the M&O caveat before he asks it.** It's your dad's shop, you run their marketing, it's a real catalog with real revenue but it is not an arm's-length customer and not a case study. It proved the tool works. Anyone calling it a client is overselling. *Saying this unprompted buys you credit you'll spend later.*
- 100 brands emailed across four batches in three weeks. Eleven niches, sourcing/verification/personalisation pipeline built. **→ your reply number here.**
- MVP half-built: catalog capture, product picker, question generation. Scan loop and dashboard not finished.
- Zero customers. Zero logged founder interviews.

**Tone target:** he should think *"he knows exactly what he does and doesn't have."*

### 0:10–0:20 — The demo. Live, not slides.

This is the most persuasive ten minutes available to you. Sequence:

1. Open ChatGPT. Type a spec-shaped buyer question in a category — *"3-seater sofa with removable washable covers under €1,500"*. Products get named and ranked.
2. Open one of those product pages. The facts are stated.
3. Open an M&O product page. The facts aren't written down anywhere. The Calira Sofa says "100% Polyester" and nothing about whether the cover comes off.
4. Then the score: 16.8/100.

That's the whole thesis in four moves, live, with nothing to take on trust.

**Then the contrast that sharpens it** (from the 3 Aug query-shape test): ask a *taste*-shaped question — "furniture brands with a warm minimalist feel" — and only brands come back, no products. The unit of the answer is set by the query, not the category. That finding is why the pitch is "win the spec-shaped queries in your category" rather than "AI visibility," and it self-qualifies customers.

**Rehearse this tonight.** Run both queries once so you know they behave. A demo that stalls live costs you more than skipping it.

### 0:20–0:35 — Why now, and why e-commerce specifically

**Why now** — one table, sourced, Adobe across 1T+ visits to US retail:

| Signal | Figure |
|---|---|
| AI referral traffic to US retail, Q1 2026 | +393% YoY |
| AI traffic conversion vs other channels, Mar 2026 | **+42% better** |
| The same comparison, Mar 2025 | **38% worse** |
| Revenue per visit, AI vs non-AI | +37% |
| Consumers who have shopped via AI | 39% |

The line to land: *"38% worse to 42% better in twelve months. That reversal is why this is a business now and wasn't last year."*

**Then immediately give him the counterweight yourself:** AI is still under 5% of most brands' traffic. This is a channel that is arriving, not one that has arrived. It changes what you can honestly promise and how you price. — He was going to find this. You saying it first is worth more than the point costs.

**Why e-commerce, and not brand-level AEO** — four arguments, in order of strength:

1. **The data is public and enumerable.** `/products.json` is open on every Shopify store. You can audit any prospect cold — no access, no login, no call, no sales cycle. The lead magnet builds itself and scales to as many brands as you can process. Almost no B2B software has this.
2. **The unit is the SKU.** A 2,000-SKU catalog is 2,000 chances to be invisible, and it's unfixable by hand — which is exactly why it's worth paying for. Brand-level AEO is one homepage and a blog; an intern does that in a week.
3. **The queries are fact-shaped, so there's something to fix.** Dimensions, material, capacity, load rating, care. Brand-level AEO is content marketing with a new name, and that market is crowded and undifferentiated.
4. **AI shopping surfaces are commercialising fastest** — ChatGPT shopping, Google's UCP standard, Amazon Rufus. You're not creating demand for the channel; the buying intent is already flowing through it.

**The competitive claim — use this wording, don't improve on it:**

> *Nobody combines AI-retrievability-specific diagnosis with remediation, at mid-market DTC scale. The AEO platforms score but don't write. The enrichment vendors write but don't score against what AI actually gets asked. We do both, at product level, for brands too small for Zoovu and too big to hand-fix.*

**And the one checkable fact that makes it stick** — say this, because he can verify it in thirty seconds and it survives:

> *Everyone selling product-level AEO at mid-market prices caps the number of SKUs. Glara tracks 20 products on Starter, 50 on Advanced. Alhena optimises 25–50 product pages a month. At 2,000 SKUs a merchant either buys enterprise or leaves most of the catalog uncovered. Whole-catalog coverage at a mid-market price is the gap.*

If he asks who else is in the space, name them without flinching: Profound ($1B val), Scrunch (acquired ~$225M), ReFiBuy ($13.6M seed), Glara, Azoma, Yotpo Discover, Alhena, plus shopping modules at Semrush and Searchable. **Knowing the landscape cold is more persuasive than the space being empty.** A founder who can name eight competitors and still explain the gap reads as serious. A founder who says "nobody does this" reads as someone who hasn't looked.

### 0:35–0:45 — The three things that aren't proven

Lead him here rather than letting him find it. Say it as: *"here are the three things that would make this not work."*

1. **That mid-market DTC brands care enough to pay.** Zero customers, zero logged conversations. This is a well-researched hypothesis, not a validated business.
2. **That remediation actually moves AI visibility.** Nobody has demonstrated this — not you, not Profound, not Yotpo, not any funded competitor.
3. **That the ROI works today rather than in two years.** At a $150k/mo brand, doubling AI visibility is roughly +$4k/mo against a $3k/mo fee. Thin. The bet is on trajectory.

**Then turn #2 over — this is the strongest thing you have to offer an engineer:**

> *"Number two is the open question in the entire category. $200M+ of venture money has gone into companies that measure whether you're visible, and not one of them has publicly shown that fixing your catalog changes it. The instrument that answers it is a re-audit diff: score a catalog, fix it, score it again, and keep the runs. That already exists — audits are written to timestamped files and never overwritten. Whoever answers that first has the only durable asset in this market, because everything else here is copyable or fundable."*

For a good engineer, *"there's an unanswered empirical question and we have the instrument"* is a far better offer than *"help me build a dashboard."* And it's true, which is why it's the right thing to lead with.

### 0:45–0:55 — Why the two of you

Concrete, not flattery. He'll discount anything that sounds like recruiting.

**What you bring:** distribution that already runs. Not "I'm good at sales" — 100 emails, four batches, eleven niches, three weeks, plus the sourcing, verification and personalisation pipeline behind it. It works today and it can feed him a stream of real catalogs indefinitely.

**What's missing, said plainly:** *"I can build enough to prove a point. I can't build the thing this becomes. That's the gap and it's why I'm asking you."*

**Why the pair is the unlock — the actual argument:**

> *"The open question is empirical, so answering it needs two things at once: someone who can build the measurement loop properly, and someone who keeps real catalogs and real merchants coming in to run it against. One of those alone spends a year learning the other. Most companies in this space are engineers who built a good tool and can't get it in front of a merchant. Ours would be the opposite problem, solved."*

**Then ask the question that decides everything, and ask it directly:**

> *"You're already building something. What's your honest read on it, and can you actually carry both?"*

Ask it early enough that he has to answer. If the answer is no and he doesn't say so now, you find out in six weeks having built nothing. **A clear "not now" on Sunday is a better outcome than a soft yes.** Say that to him — it lowers the cost of honesty and makes the yes worth more.

### 0:55–1:05 — The ask

Be specific. Vagueness here reads as not-seriousness.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Engineering + product/delivery. His current "strategy" slot becomes a real function with output attached — the brief flags that as unresolved and Sunday resolves it. |

**On 51/49 — say why, don't let him wonder:**

> *"Near-equal, deliberately. I hold the extra point because deadlock kills companies and someone has to be able to break a tie. Everything else — direction, vesting, what we build, how we sell it — is a conversation between equals, and if I'm ever using that 1% to win an argument, something has already gone wrong."*

**Decide tonight what you do if he asks for 50/50.** Don't improvise it in the room. Reasonable positions: hold at 51 and explain deadlock again; or offer 50/50 with a written tie-break mechanism. Either is defensible. Deciding live is not.

### 1:05–1:20 — Hand him the arguments

Two questions are genuinely open. Give them to him and let him take a side. This is the part that converts.

1. **Service now that becomes software, or a services company built by accident?** Your instinct is manual work first because it's how you learn which fixes matter. His instinct may be to build the agent immediately. That tension is written in `00-BRIEF.md` §10 as unresolved — say it's unresolved and that you want him to argue it.
2. **Does a 6–12 month window mean move fast, or mean don't start?** Yotpo Discover is e-commerce-native, SKU-level, currently targeting $10M+ GMV and publicly planning a down-market version. You'd be renting this position, not owning it.

**Close by asking:** *"What's the biggest reason not to do this?"* — then stop talking. Whatever he says next is the real objection, and it's more useful than anything you say in the last five minutes.

## Do not say

Every one of these is disprovable by someone with a phone, and he has a phone.

- ❌ **"Nobody does this for e-commerce"** / "nobody does product-level" / "nobody has locked down mid-market." All false. Glara sells product-level attribute auditing to mid-market DTC right now at $120–345/mo. Use the wording in the run sheet verbatim. Softening it to "almost nobody" is the same mistake with a hedge on it.
- ❌ **"Any sentence of the form 'nobody has X'"** without a source.
- ❌ **M&O as a customer, client, or case study.** Test catalog. Say whose shop it is, unprompted.
- ❌ **Searchable's "$85M valuation" or "$14M raise."** `00-BRIEF.md` §4 still carries those figures; the newer `competitors/LANDSCAPE.md` (24 Aug) traces the verifiable raise to €3.4M in Dec 2025 and treats the rest as unverified marketing. Newer file wins. If it comes up, say €3.4M or say you don't know.

- ❌ **The audit tool as "a product."** It's a script that writes a markdown file.

## Reading the room

**Real yes:** argues with you on specifics. Asks about the split before you raise it. Starts scoping the build unprompted. Pushes back hard on the window or on service-vs-agent. Says "we."

**Polite no wearing a yes:** "sounds cool." "Keep me posted." "Let me think about it" with no questions attached. Agrees with everything. Asks nothing about equity.

If it's the second, don't chase it in the room — take the "not now," ask what would have to be true for it to be a yes in three months, and keep him as the friend who helps. That's a materially better outcome than a co-founder who vests for a year and disappears.
