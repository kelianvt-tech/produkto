# Startup Playbook

A repeatable sequence for taking a startup idea from nothing to a funded, revenue-generating launch, derived from a founder who used it to build four businesses past 8 figures across four industries (most recently: $0 to $3M ARR and an $85M valuation in 5 months).

Use this skill any time the user is at an early stage of building a company — choosing co-founders, deciding if an idea is worth pursuing, sizing a market, scoping an MVP, planning how to get first customers, or building a go-to-market/content plan. Don't wait for them to say "playbook" — questions like "is this a good idea," "how do I validate this," "how do I find a co-founder," or "how should I launch this" are all cues to apply this framework.

The core discipline behind every step: don't build in silence, and don't scale a guess. Prove demand exists at each stage before investing more time in the next one. Walk the user through steps in order — each step's output is the input to the next (e.g., you can't write good waitlist content until you've validated the idea; you can't scope an MVP well until you know what your waitlist actually wants).

## Step 1: Assemble a founding team around three skills

Most fast-growing companies start as a small team, not a solo founder (Zuckerberg + roommates, Jobs + Wozniak + Markkula). If the user is deciding who to bring on, help them check that these three functions are covered — by different people if possible, since each is close to a full-time job:

1. **Product/UX** — owns the actual experience the customer gets. Not just "make it work," but make it a product people are drawn to. Whoever owns this should have final say over what gets built and why, not just how.
2. **Engineering** — owns the architecture and makes the product technically real. Distinct from #1: one shapes what the product should feel like, the other makes it exist and scale.
3. **Sales/marketing/distribution** — owns the story and the growth engine. This person is usually the public face of the company and drives everything in Step 6.

If the user is a solo founder, don't tell them to abandon ship — but flag which of the three functions they're weakest in and help them think about how to cover it (a co-founder, an early hire, or an agency as a stopgap). The team doesn't need to be found via a dramatic cross-country search — colleagues and existing network connections work fine, what matters is the skill coverage and shared conviction in the idea.

## Step 2: Validate the idea with CODE

Before writing a line of code or a business plan, pressure-test the idea across four dimensions. Don't let the user commit real time to an idea until it's been through all four — a great team can build almost anything, so the risk isn't capability, it's picking the wrong thing to build.

**C — Consumer trends.** Is there a genuine, current shift in consumer behavior, technology, or demand — a "rising tide"? Help the user articulate the specific shift, not just "AI is big" but something concrete and observable (e.g., "people are asking ChatGPT instead of Google to find services").

**O — Opportunity.** Within that trend, what specific, unaddressed problem sits at the intersection of where the trend is heading and what nobody's solved yet? This is narrower than the trend itself — push the user past "AI search matters" toward "there's no tool that gets businesses discovered inside AI chat answers."

**D — Demand.** Is there already an active, ongoing conversation among the target customer about this exact problem? Have the user (or help them) go looking in forums, subreddits, LinkedIn comments, review sites, industry reports — the goal is finding people already complaining, not writing a hypothetical survey. Two important reframes to give the user if they resist this step:
- **Competitors are a good sign, not a bad one.** If other businesses already solve this problem with an older method (e.g., SEO tools existing validates that AEO/AI-search tools have a market), that's proof budget already exists. Total absence of competition is a yellow flag, not a green one.
- If people are "wherever you look... asking the same questions constantly and no one has a clear answer," that's demand — you don't need a formal survey to prove it further.

**E — Economic sizing.** Compute total addressable market: `(total potential customers) × (total spend per customer per year)`. Walk the user through this explicitly with real numbers, the same way you'd fact-check any market-sizing claim — don't accept a guess, look up actual counts and actual price points. Example structure to reuse:
- How many potential customer accounts exist? (e.g., "58,000 care homes in the US/UK")
- What do they currently spend on solutions like this, per year? (e.g., "$3,000/month → $36,000/year")
- Multiply. Look for markets where a small percentage share is still a big business — avoid markets so small that the user would need to dominate them outright to make good money.

Only move to Step 3 once all four hold up. If one is weak (e.g., great trend, no visible demand yet), say so plainly and help the user either narrow the idea or keep looking rather than plowing ahead.

## Step 3: Build an audience before building the product

The single most common mistake at this stage is building in silence for months and only trying to generate interest at launch. Instead, help the user start building an audience the moment the idea is validated, using a waitlist as the mechanism:

- Publish genuinely useful, educational content about the problem space — not sales content, not pitching the (unbuilt) product. Teach the audience something real about their problem.
- Put a single, low-friction call to action at the end of each piece: sign up to be first to know when it launches.
- Why a waitlist specifically: asking someone to hand over an email address with zero financial commitment converts at roughly 50–70%, versus 2–5% for trying to sell directly. It's the highest-leverage way to capture real intent cheaply.
- Rough health check: if the user can't get ~200 people onto a waitlist, that's a signal the CODE validation in Step 2 may have been too optimistic — flag this rather than letting them push forward blind.
- This works regardless of existing audience size — the mechanism (educate → capture intent → nurture) is the same whether they have 100 followers or 100,000.

## Step 4: Build one narrow MVP — not a product

The second most common failure mode: spending months building a full product nobody has validated wants. Push back hard if the user starts describing a roadmap instead of a single feature.

The instruction to give: **pick one feature, for one ideal customer, that solves one specific problem.** That's the whole MVP. Deliberately, almost aggressively simple — the goal isn't to impress anyone, it's to get a real user's hands on something so they start telling you what to build next.

Ask the user: "What's the one thing you and your team could ship right now — not in two months — that would prove this idea works?" Help them strip scope until the answer is genuinely small enough to build in days or a couple of weeks, not months.

## Step 5: Launch early access and build a feedback flywheel

Once the MVP exists, open it to a slice of the waitlist — not the whole list — often paired with an early-access incentive (e.g., a lifetime discount) to attract product champions rather than casual signups. The point of the incentive isn't revenue at this stage; it's getting people who will actually use the product daily and tell you what's wrong with it.

Help the user set up the flywheel mechanics:
- A tight, daily loop: collect feedback (what's working, confusing, broken, missing) → ship fixes/improvements fast → users see responsiveness → they trust the product more and refer others → repeat.
- A shared space (Slack/Discord community, etc.) where early users can talk to each other and to the founders, which compounds the feedback loop beyond 1:1 conversations.
- Direct, high-volume founder time on calls with users during this phase — this is meant to be intensive and doesn't scale, and that's fine; it's temporary.

## Step 6: Build a content machine (three layers, run together)

This should start in parallel with product work, not after — bolting on marketing right before launch creates unnecessary pressure. Help the user build out all three layers:

1. **Founder content.** Consistent, personal posting (LinkedIn or wherever the ICP spends time) about the problem being solved, mistakes made, lessons learned. People engage with a person's perspective far more than a brand's — by the time a prospect finds the product, they already trust the founder's thinking, and the sale is half-done. Content should map directly to specific ICP pain points, not generic "thought leadership."
2. **GTM/outreach.** Convert warm waitlist/content leads into demo calls (low friction, since trust is already partly built). Layer in personalized cold outreach for prospects not yet in the funnel — the pattern to use: lead with a specific, free diagnosis of their situation ("I looked at X and see three opportunities around [problem]... happy to walk you through it") rather than a generic pitch or demo ask.
3. **Answer/search visibility (AEO/SEO).** Make sure the brand actually shows up where the ICP looks for answers now — this may mean traditional SEO, but increasingly means showing up in AI chat answers (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, etc.) too. Treat this as a compounding asset that gets more expensive to catch up on the longer it's ignored.

## Step 7: Build a high-value lead magnet

To convert traffic and interest into actual accounts, help the user create one genuinely valuable, free asset — a tool, report, framework, or dataset the ICP can't easily get elsewhere — gated behind a small exchange (email, or an account signup with no credit card required). This is the mechanism that turns anonymous traffic into an owned relationship the user can keep marketing to directly.

The strongest version of this pattern: make the free tool itself diagnostic and personalized to the visitor (e.g., "enter your company name and we'll show you exactly how you compare to competitors on X") rather than a generic downloadable PDF — personalized, visitor-specific outputs convert dramatically better than static lead magnets.

## Applying this with a user

When someone brings you an idea or a "should I do this" question, don't just narrate the framework — actually run it with them:

- For idea validation: ask for the specifics needed to fill in each CODE letter, and do the TAM math out loud with real numbers rather than accepting "it's a big market."
- For launch planning: turn the seven steps into a concrete, dated project plan tailored to their situation — call out what's realistic to skip or compress if they're pre-team or pre-funding.
- For content/waitlist/lead-magnet asks: produce actual draft copy or concepts following the patterns above, not just a description of the pattern.
- Be willing to tell the user an idea, an MVP scope, or a market size doesn't hold up — the whole point of this framework is killing bad ideas cheaply, early, and validating good ones with real signal rather than optimism.
