# Interview guide

**First call:** Ben Attwood, Clubhouse Skin. Sunday 17:00 London / 18:00 Madrid. He's calling, +44 7311 860868.

Keep this open. Tick questions off as you go.

---

## Before you dial (10 minutes)

- Read his Shopify article again. One detail you can mention naturally.
- Open his site. Click two product pages. Note whether ingredients, size, skin type, usage are stated as fields or buried in prose. **Do not raise this unless he does.** It's for your notes.
- Have this file open and somewhere to type.
- **Ask to record in the first 30 seconds.** You cannot capture exact phrasing while also holding a conversation, and exact phrasing is the entire point.

**Know this going in:** Clubhouse Skin has 6 products. He could fix his whole catalog in an afternoon, so he is not a customer and never will be. That is fine. **He is a data point, not a lead.** It also means you can be completely relaxed, which will make the conversation better.

---

## The one rule

**Ask what they have done, never what they would do.**

"Would you pay for this?" gets a polite yes and teaches you nothing. "What did you do last time?" gets the truth, because it already happened.

If you catch yourself explaining your idea, stop and ask another question. You are not here to be understood.

## Structure, 20 minutes

### 1. Open (2 min)

> "Thanks for doing this. Quick context: I'm at IE in Madrid, writing a report on how e-commerce founders are thinking about AI search. No agenda beyond that. Mind if I record so I can quote you properly rather than paraphrasing badly?"

Then let him talk. Do not fill silences.

### 2. Their world (5 min)

Warm-up, but genuinely useful. You're mapping who does what.

- How long have you been running Clubhouse Skin, and how big is the team now?
- Who touches the website day to day?
- Where do most of your customers come from at the moment?
- **Who writes the product pages? When were they last properly rewritten?**

*Listen for:* whether anyone owns product content, or whether it's nobody's job. That's the structural claim your whole business rests on.

### 3. How they think about search (6 min)

Do not say "AEO" or "GEO". Use plain words and let him supply his own.

- Has anything changed in how people find you in the last year or two?
- Do you do anything for search? Who handles it?
- **Have you ever typed your own product into ChatGPT to see what happens?**
- Do you think about AI search at all, or is it not on the list yet?
- **When you think about AI and your brand, are you picturing your brand name coming up, or a specific product coming up?**

*Listen for:* the last question is no longer the wedge test it was — **brands do picture specific products** (settled 19 Aug 2026, `context/ICP.md`). Still ask it, and still write the answer down verbatim, but for his *vocabulary*, not to decide whether the positioning survives. The page's weight moves to §4, money and past behaviour.

*Also capture:* the exact words he uses. "AI search"? "ChatGPT"? "the robots"? Something else? That vocabulary becomes your subject lines.

### 4. Money and effort, actual past behaviour (5 min)

The sharp part. Stay casual.

- Have you spent money on anything search or AI related? What happened?
- What's the last thing you paid an agency or freelancer for on the website?
- Walk me through the last time you had to update a lot of product pages at once. What did that actually take?
- If someone showed you a report saying your products were invisible to ChatGPT, what would you do with it? *(Watch for: "nothing, honestly." That's a real answer and a valuable one.)*
- What's actually top of your list this quarter?

*Listen for:* where money already flows. People pay for things adjacent to what they already pay for.

### 5. The product question (4 min)

**Ask this last, never earlier.** Once you describe the tool, everything after it is contaminated: he'll answer to be supportive rather than accurately. Get the behavioural answers banked first.

Be straight about why you're asking. It's true, and it gives him permission to say no:

> "Last thing, and it's the selfish one. I'm thinking about building something and I'm interviewing store owners to work out whether there's actually demand for it, so an honest no is genuinely more useful to me than a polite yes.
>
> The idea is a tool that shows you, product by product, how visible each of your products is in AI search, and how it ranks against your competitors' products for the same questions. So not 'is my brand mentioned' but 'when someone asks ChatGPT for a face serum for oily skin, does mine come up, and where does it sit against the ones that do'.
>
> Is that interesting to you or not really?"

Then stop talking and let him answer properly. Do not rescue the silence.

**Scope, so you describe it consistently:** visibility and competitive ranking per product. That's it. Not fixing anything, not writing content, not an AI assistant. If he asks "does it fix it too?" the honest answer is "not in the first version, it just shows you where you stand" — and then ask whether that's a problem for him, because his answer to *that* is worth a lot.

**The follow-ups are where the information is. The first yes is worth nothing.**

- What would you actually use it for?
- Have you seen anything that does this already?  ← *tests whether the category has reached him at all. If he names Profound, Semrush or Peec, that is a very different world from never having heard of it.*
- If it told you product 3 was invisible, what would you actually do next?  ← *the measurement-only question. If the honest answer is "nothing", a dashboard doesn't help him.*
- What would you expect something like that to cost?  ← *let him name the number first*
- If it were €300 a month, would that be an easy yes, a maybe, or a no?  ← *a real number forces a real reaction*
- What would you stop paying for to fund it?
- Who else would need to agree?

**Then the question that matters more than any of them:**

> "It doesn't exist properly yet. If I got a version working, would you want to be one of the first to try it?"

*Asking for a small real commitment beats any hypothetical.* If he says yes, ask what a good time to come back would be, and put it in your calendar in front of him. A vague "sure, keep me posted" is a no wearing a polite hat.

**How to read it:**

| What he does | What it means |
|---|---|
| Asks when it'll be ready, or can he see it | Genuine interest |
| Names a price without you prompting | Genuine interest |
| Asks who else uses it | Genuine interest |
| Says "yeah that's really useful" and nothing follows | Politeness. Not a signal. |
| Says he'd have to think about it | No |
| Says no and explains why | **The most valuable answer available.** Ask him to keep going. |

Remember he has 6 products. His answer to the pay question is close to meaningless on its own, because he has no scale problem. What is meaningful is whether he finds the *idea* interesting, and the words he uses to describe why or why not.

### 6. Close (2 min)

- Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?
- Who else runs a store and would be worth me talking to? **Ask this every time.** Referrals are the cheapest leads you will ever get.

Then thank him and get off the call on time. Ending at 20 minutes when you said 20 minutes is its own kind of credibility.

**Do not promise to send him anything.** No report, no audit, no follow-up material. You have not committed to it and you should not create an obligation you may not want later.

---

## Do not

- **Do not describe the tool before section 5.** If he asks earlier, say "I'll come back to that at the end if that's alright, I don't want to lead your answers" and move on. That line is true and it makes you sound like you know what you're doing.
- **Do not pitch even in section 5.** Describing a mechanic and asking if it's interesting is a question. Listing benefits is a pitch. Stay on the first side of that line.
- **Do not offer to audit his catalog.** Six products. It would be transparently a pretext.
- **Do not promise to send him anything.**
- **Do not defend the idea** if he's sceptical. Scepticism is the most useful thing you can get. Ask him to expand on it.
- **Do not lead.** "Don't you think it's a problem that…" produces agreement, not information.
