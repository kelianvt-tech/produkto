## Part 1 — The strategy

### The one-line version

Donnelly owns the *definition* of AI search. We own the *diagnosis*. He explains the category from 30,000 feet to 1.2M people; nobody is publishing product-page-level data on what's actually missing from real catalogs. That is the only ground where a pre-validation founder can beat an eight-figure competitor, so everything below points at it.

### Revised pillar order

Changed from the skill's original order after the Donnelly teardown. Category explainers are demoted — that ground is taken.

| # | Pillar | Weekly slots | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Teardowns and diagnostics** | 2 | The wedge. Real audit data nobody else has. Uncopyable. |
| **2** | Why catalogs are invisible | 1 | The mechanism. Converts vague fear into a fixable problem. |
| **3** | AI is becoming a shopping channel | 1 | The stakes. Adobe data, always with the counterweight. |
| **4** | Building in public | 1 | Trust. The only pillar that runs on zero new data. |

### The weekly rhythm

| Day | Pillar | Type | Format |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Shopping channel | Educational | Sheet |
| Tue | Teardown | Educational | Sheet |
| Wed | Building in public | Story | Text only |
| Thu | Catalogs invisible | Educational or lead magnet | Sheet |
| Fri | Teardown | Lead magnet or conversion | Sheet or text |

Thursday is Donnelly's heaviest day and the teardown data suggests it's the strongest slot generally — put the lead magnet there.

Over a month that lands near the target mix: ~50% educational, ~20% story, ~20% lead magnet, ~10% conversion.

**Format rule from the teardown:** sheets carry volume (85% of his posts, 870 avg reactions) but his text-only posts average the most (1,136). Post the sheet when the value is a *list or framework*; post plain text when the idea stands alone. Wednesday stays text on purpose.

### The engine: comments, not likes

The single most transferable mechanic. Donnelly runs a **61% comment-to-reaction ratio against a ~6% platform norm** by gating a genuinely useful resource behind one word.

Our version is stronger than his, because the resource is bespoke rather than a PDF:

> **"Comment AUDIT and I'll run your catalog and send you the report. Free, no pitch attached."**

This does three jobs at once — drives comment velocity, captures a lead, and *is* the demand validation the business currently needs. The 20-cold-audits milestone gets hit as a byproduct of posting.

Two rungs on the ladder:

- **Low friction:** the attribute checklist PDF, gated by email. For people not ready to hand over their store URL.
- **High friction, high intent:** the live audit. Every person who comments is a qualified conversation.

**Cap it.** Each audit is real work. Run the gated-audit post once a week at most, and if volume exceeds capacity, say so publicly — "I can do five this week, first five comments" — which adds scarcity honestly rather than manufacturing it.

### Engagement — 30 minutes before posting, not after

The 30/30/30/10 split, with one amendment:

- **30% ICP** — DTC founders and heads of e-commerce in spec-driven categories.
- **30% larger creators** — ⚠️ **not Donnelly on AEO topics.** He's a competitor. Commenting under his AEO explainers puts us in his funnel where he sets the frame. Engage on his LinkedIn-strategy posts if at all. Better targets: e-commerce operators, Shopify ecosystem voices, retail analysts.
- **30% peers** — e-commerce, SEO, agency people. Agencies may become a channel rather than a threat; worth testing.
- **10% friends.**

Comments must add an insight, ask something real, share experience, or disagree with reasoning. On our own posts: reply to everything in the first 30 minutes, and reply to the specific point made.

### Hooks — open on a number

Every one of Donnelly's top posts leads with a hard figure. Ours available today:

- 16.8 / 100 · 86 products · 6 attributes on zero products
- 66% machine-readable (Adobe, worst of any retail page type)
- +393% YoY AI referral traffic · 42% better conversion · 39% of consumers
- Under 5% of traffic — the counterweight, and a hook in its own right

**Do not use the credibility-flex or milestone-reveal hooks.** No exit, no milestones. A weak version of those reads as posturing.

### 90-day scoreboard

Judge on the left column. The right column is vanity until month 4.

### MONDAY — "The conversion number flipped"

> Last March, AI traffic converted 38% worse than every other channel.
>
> This March, it converted 42% better.
>
> That's Adobe, across a trillion visits to US retail sites. In twelve months the worst-converting traffic source became the best one.
>
> Here's why it matters more than the growth number everyone quotes:
>
> AI referral traffic to retail is up 393% year on year. Impressive, and slightly misleading — it's growing fast because it started at nearly zero.
>
> The conversion flip is the real signal. It means the people arriving from ChatGPT aren't browsing. They've already asked their questions, already narrowed it down, and they arrive at the page ready.
>
> Revenue per visit is 37% higher.
>
> Now the part nobody selling you AI visibility tools will say out loud:
>
> This is still under 5% of most brands' traffic.
>
> It is not where your revenue comes from this quarter. Anyone telling you to panic is selling something.
>
> But it's the highest-intent traffic on the internet, growing at triple digits, and most catalogs are structurally invisible to it.
>
> That's a cheap position to take now and an expensive one to take in 2028.
>
> What's your read — is anyone actually seeing AI traffic in their analytics yet?

**Type:** Educational · **Pillar:** AI as a shopping channel · **Hook:** shocking stat (contrarian flip)
**Why it works:** the reversal is inherently surprising, and the "under 5%" counterweight buys credibility that the rest of the category spends.

### TUESDAY — "I scored a real catalog and it got 16.8"

> I ran my audit tool against a real furniture catalog last month.
>
> 86 products. Score: 16.8 out of 100.
>
> The score isn't the interesting part. This is:
>
> Six attributes that buyers actively search on appeared on ZERO products. Not on some. On none.
>
> → Care instructions
> → Indoor/outdoor suitability
> → Removable covers
> → Load capacity
> → Assembly required
> → Fabric durability
>
> Now picture a buyer asking ChatGPT:
>
> "A sofa with removable covers I can put on a covered terrace."
>
> That's a completely normal question. It's also unanswerable from this catalog — not because the sofas fail the criteria, but because nobody ever wrote down whether they meet it.
>
> The products are good. The photography is beautiful. The pages convert fine for humans.
>
> And an AI reading them has nothing to match against.
>
> This is the bit most brands get wrong: they assume being invisible to AI is a technical problem. Schema markup, structured data, some plugin.
>
> It isn't. It's a *content* problem. The facts were never stated, because for ten years nobody needed them stated — you wrote for humans who could see the photo, and for Google, which ranked the page not the product.
>
> Being fair about this: it's one catalog, and it's a shop I know well rather than an arm's-length client. One data point isn't a pattern.
>
> But Adobe scored product detail pages across US retail at 66% machine-readable — the worst of any page type on a retail site. So I doubt it's an outlier.
>
> If you sell something with specs, open your best-selling product page right now and look for those six lines.
>
> I'd genuinely like to know what you find.

**Type:** Educational · **Pillar:** Teardown (lead pillar) · **Hook:** original-data drop
**Why it works:** the number is ours, the six-attribute list is concrete and savable, and the self-imposed caveat is disarming in a category of overclaimers. The closing ask costs the reader ten seconds and produces replies.

**🖼 Image spec — scorecard, 1200×1350**
Top third: huge numeral **16.8** with `/100` small beside it, and beneath in caps *"SPEC-QUERY RETRIEVABILITY · 86 PRODUCTS."* Bottom two-thirds: the six attributes as a vertical checklist, each with a hollow/empty state marker and the label **"0 of 86 products"** right-aligned in a muted tone. Bold sans-serif, one accent colour on the 16.8 only, everything else near-black on off-white. Deliberately austere — it should read like a diagnostic printout, not marketing.

### WEDNESDAY — "I built the thing before I knew anyone wanted it"

> I built the tool before I'd spoken to a single customer.
>
> Classic mistake. I knew it was a mistake while I was doing it.
>
> Here's the honest reason: building was the comfortable part. I could sit with the code and feel productive, and every hour spent there was an hour I wasn't asking a stranger whether they'd pay for this.
>
> So the tool works now. It crawls a catalog, scores whether products state the facts buyers ask about, and tells you which questions you're invisible for.
>
> And I have zero customers.
>
> What I've got instead is one scored catalog, a pile of market data, and three things I can't yet prove:
>
> 1. That brands care enough about this to pay for it
> 2. That fixing the data actually moves whether AI recommends you
> 3. That the return works this year rather than in 2028
>
> Number two is the one that keeps me up. Nobody has demonstrated it publicly. Not me, not the funded companies in this space with real money behind them. Everyone is selling a dashboard and hoping the correlation holds.
>
> I'm not going to pretend I've solved it.
>
> What I'm doing instead: running the audit free on twenty catalogs and sending the report with nothing attached. No pitch, no call booked, no funnel.
>
> If twenty brands read a report showing exactly what their catalog is missing and none of them care, that's my answer. It's a cheap way to find out I'm wrong, and finding out early is worth more than being right slowly.
>
> Nine to go.
>
> If you run a DTC catalog with more than a few hundred products, message me and I'll add you to the list.

**Type:** Personal story · **Pillar:** Building in public · **Hook:** contrarian confession
**Why it works:** vulnerability with a specific number attached, not vague founder-therapy. Naming the unproven link is exactly what the honesty rules demand and it differentiates hard from competitors. Ends on a soft, non-gated ask.
**Format:** text only, no image — this is the day to use his highest-performing format.

### THURSDAY — The lead magnet

> Most brands trying to fix AI visibility start with schema markup.
>
> It's the wrong end of the problem, and it's why the effort usually doesn't move anything.
>
> Schema tells a machine what *kind* of thing your page describes. It doesn't help if the fact itself was never written on the page.
>
> You can have flawless structured data and still be unrecommendable, because "does this work outdoors?" isn't a schema field. It's a sentence somebody has to write.
>
> So I put together the checklist I actually run when I audit a catalog.
>
> 14 attributes, grouped by what buyers ask about:
>
> → Physical — dimensions, weight, capacity, load rating
> → Material — composition, finish, durability rating
> → Use context — indoor/outdoor, room type, age suitability
> → Ownership — care, cleaning, removable parts, assembly, warranty
>
> Against each one: how to phrase it so a model can actually parse it, and the most common way brands state it that doesn't work.
>
> That last column is the useful bit. "Easy to care for" is not a care instruction. "Machine washable at 30°C, removable cover" is.
>
> It's free and there's nothing attached to it.
>
> Comment CHECKLIST and I'll send it over.
>
> (If you'd rather skip the checklist and just see your own catalog scored — say AUDIT instead and I'll run it and send you the report.)

**Type:** Lead magnet · **Pillar:** Why catalogs are invisible · **Hook:** contrarian correction
**Why it works:** the schema misconception is a real, widely-held belief, so correcting it earns the right to the offer. Two-rung ladder in one post catches both low- and high-intent readers.

**📋 You need to build this first.** The 14-attribute checklist doesn't exist yet — the post is unpostable without it. It's a 2–3 page PDF: attribute, why buyers ask, how to phrase it, common failure. Say the word and I'll draft it.

### FRIDAY — "Everyone sells you the dashboard"

> There are at least five companies that will tell you your products are invisible to AI.
>
> Profound. Scrunch. Alhena. Searchable. Semrush now too.
>
> They're good tools, run by smart people, and they all do the same thing: measure your visibility and show it to you on a dashboard.
>
> Then what?
>
> Genuine question. Picture a 20-person DTC brand with 2,000 products. The dashboard says 1,400 of them are missing attributes buyers search on.
>
> Who fixes them?
>
> Not the founder — they're running the business. Not the marketing hire — they're on paid and email. Not the agency — they're contracted for ads and their scope doesn't cover rewriting your PDPs.
>
> Nobody at that company has "action the visibility dashboard across 2,000 products" in their job description. So the dashboard gets opened, screenshotted for a board deck, and never opened again.
>
> The measurement is real. The measurement is also the easy half.
>
> The hard half is 2,000 products × 14 attributes = 28,000 facts that have to be researched, written, and pushed live without breaking the product feed.
>
> That's the part I do. I audit it, then I actually go and fix it.
>
> I'm early and I'm honest about what I can't prove yet — whether remediation reliably moves AI recommendations is not settled by anyone in this space, me included. What I can tell you is that a catalog which doesn't state the facts definitely can't be matched against them.
>
> If you've got a few hundred products or more and you want to know where you stand before you spend anything: comment AUDIT and I'll run yours and send the report.
>
> Free. No call, no pitch.

**Type:** Conversion · **Pillar:** Teardown / positioning · **Hook:** contrarian correction
**Why it works:** names competitors respectfully — which reads as confidence, not attack — then finds the gap they leave. The "who fixes them?" question does the selling. Honesty clause defuses the strongest objection before it's raised.

**🖼 Image spec — comparison, 1200×1200**
Two columns. Left header **"WHAT THEY SELL"** with three items: *Measurement · A dashboard · A list of problems.* Right header **"WHAT ACTUALLY NEEDS DOING"** with three items: *2,000 products · 14 attributes each · 28,000 facts written and shipped.* Left column deliberately sparse with lots of empty space beneath; right column dense and full. The visual asymmetry is the argument. Same accent colour on the right only.

⚠️ **Check before posting:** naming Searchable means naming Chris Donnelly's company, and his account has 1.2M followers. If he or his team see it, expect a reply. That's not a reason to avoid it — being visibly unafraid of the incumbent is good positioning — but go in knowing, and have a substantive answer ready rather than a defensive one.
